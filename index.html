Introducing the Swiss Cheese Model

coined by James T. Reason

das sogenannter Schweizer-Käse-Modell, welches von James Reason in den 2000ern geprägt wurde. Dieser James Reason ist im Prinzip der Urvater von modernem Fehlermanagement und -Kultur.

the so-called Swiss Cheese Model, which has been originally propounded in the 2000s by James Reason, who basically is the Godfather of modern error management.

Das Model besagt also, dass wir als Eingangsgröße unzählige Gefahren haben, und die Scheiben des Käses verhindern, dass letztliche daraus ein Unfall passiert. Aber jede Scheibe hat Löcher, genauso wie eine Scheibe Emmentaler Löcher hat. Und wenn diese Löcher ungünstig in Deckung liegen, hat die ursprüngliche Gefahr die Möglichkeit da durchzuschlüpfen und zu einem ausgewachsenen Problem zu werden.

So the model says, we have, as inputs, a vast amount of hazards, and these slices of cheese prevent that eventually an accident happens. But every layer of defence has holes in it, just like a slice of Emmentaler has. And if those layers come to an alignment in an unfavorable manner, the initial hazard had the chance to sneak through and become a genuine accident.

Alles mögliche, egal wie unscheinbar es zu sein scheint, ist so eine Schutzschicht. Ein Berechtigungssystem, oder ein roter Aufkleber bei Knöpfen, die man gerne verwechselt oder so.

Everything, no matter how irrelevant it looks, is a layer. It could be a permission system in place, or a dyed console which clearly and loudly indicates whether you're typing in the development or production system's console.

James Reason hat Störfälle in zwei Arten unterschieden: in Aktivem und Latenten Versagen; später hat er sie eher Personen und Systemfehler genannt bzw. Unmittelbare und Zugrundeliegende Ursachen. Die Systemfehler bzw. Zugrundeliegenden Ursachen lauern erstmal versteckt und nur zum Vorschein, wenn sie untersucht werden, weil irgendwo ein Unfall passiert ist. Sie liegen in der Organisation bzw. im Management: und irgendwann passiert 'was, was nur passiert ist, weil die organisatorischen Umstände es ermöglicht haben.

James reason partitioned failures in two kinds: in "Latent failures" and "Active failures". Later, he renamed it a bit to be "underlying causes" and "immediate causes". The "underlying causes" are hidden and only come to the surface when they are examined due to an accident happened. They lie in the organization or the management. — And at some point in time an event happens, which only happens because the organizational circumstances has made it possible altogether.

Wenn ein Unfall passiert, wird die Schuld meistens bei den Agierenden ganz vorne gesucht. Aber ist es vielleicht nicht doch eher ein zugrundeliegender bzw. Systemfehler? Stellt Euch einen Softwarefehler vor. Offensichtlich ist derjenige dafür verantwortlich, der den Code dazu geschrieben hat, oder? Nein — nach unserem Modell hier hat auch die Organisation dazu beigetragen, indem sie unpassende Personalbesetzung begünstigt hat, vielleicht einen unerfahrenen mit einem erfahrenen Entwickler gemeinsam arbeiten lassen sollte, usw. — Umgekehrt schauen wir aber auch auf die Aktiven bzw. Personenfehler: unsichere Handlungen von Beteiligten, Vergesslichkeit, Unachtsamkeit, schlechte Motivation, Fahrlässigkeit, Draufgängertum usw.

When an Accident happens, the blaming is usually targeted at the players acting at the front line. But isn't it really an underlying or a system failure? Just take a stupid bug as an example. Obviously the developer is to blame who literally wrote the code no? Of course not: the organization allowed for inappropriate staffing, they should pair an experienced with an unexperienced etc. — The opposite are looking at active failures: unsafe acts or omissions of persons, Forgetfulness, inattention, poor motivation, carelessness, negligence, recklessness.

We live with Hazardous Attitudes

  • Anti-authority (“Don’t tell me”)
  • Impulsiveness (“Do it quickly”)
  • Invulnerability (“It won’t happen to me”)
  • Macho (“I can do it.”)
  • Resignation (“What’s the use?”)

Wir leben mit songenannten "Gefährlichen Grundhaltungen", dieser Begriff und die Haltungen kommmen zum Beispiel genau so aus der Pilotenausbildung. Antiautorität ("ich lass mir nichts vorschreiben"), Impulsivität ("Schnell schnell"), Unverletzbarkeit: "Mir passiert sowas nicht", Selbstüberschätzung/Imponiergehabe ("Macho"): suchen unnötige zusätzliche Risiken um anderen zu zeigen, wie toll sie sind; und Resignation ("egal was ich tue, nichts hilft", oder, "da kann man eh nichts machen")

AA: I don't let anyone tell me what to do
Impulsiveness: Act quickly, don't think long or weigh consequences
Invuln: happens to others only, why should it happen to me?
Macho: The show-off, they seek extra unnecessary risk to prove to others how great they are
Resignation: no matter what I try it doesn't help. On failure it's deduced to wrong decisions, on succes it's considered just because of luck or coincidence.

Cognitive dissonance

…kognitive Dissonanz. In unserem Falle Fehlerkultur meinen wir damit die Neigung, Dinge schönzumalen; Fakten unbewusst zu verdrehen, damit sie in unser allgemeines Bild passen, obwohl sie ein Hinweis auf einen Fehler sein können. Stellt Euch vielleicht vor, ihr steht vor Eurem Auto, versucht es aufzuschließen, es geht aber nicht. Ihr drückt den Schlüssel, drückt ihn öfter, überlegt, ob ihr die Batterien tauschen sollt oder steckt ihn ins Schloss: nichts davon hilft. Und da das Auto gestern erst im Service war, seid ihr sicher, dass es damit zusammenhängen muss und versteift Euch darauf; obwohl ihr in Wahrheit vor einem fremden Auto steht das zufällig genauso aussieht, und Eures drei Reihen weiter steht. Somit hat Euch besonders der Umstand, dass das Auto kürzlich in der Werkstatt war oder ihr es Eurem Nachwuchs geliehen hattet, auf die komplett falsche Fährte gelockt.

In our case cognitive dissonance means the habit of whitewashing things; twist facts so they fit our overall image of the situation: signs of sources of errors.

We have the chance to gain knowledge and wisdom from mistakes in order to avoid them in the future.

Mistakes must be processed

  • …with us for ourselves (mostly inherently given)
  • to tighten the "cheese hole": with the organization to give it the opportunity to learn

Wenn Fehler passieren, muss man sie bearbeiten. Einerseits "im Stillen" mit uns selbst, das passiert meistens unbewusst sowieso; und auch, um die Größe des Lochs in der entsprechenden Käsescheibe kleiner zu machen. Wir müssen es in unserer Organisation ansprechen, damit diese die Gelegenheit hat zu daraus zu lernen und Maßnahmen abzuleiten. Wir kennen solche Formate schon als Post Mortems oder Fuck-Up-Nights oder so.

Mistakes that have been done must be dealt with. On one hand "silently" with ourselves, which mostly happens unconsciously anyways; and to tighten the cheese hole of the respective slice: it must be addressed within the organisation to give the opoprtunity to learn. We all know this concept already called "post mortem" or Fuckup Nights etc.

No "Pu.Mist. culture"

Punishing mistakes and thus encouraging the covering up of mistakes prevents an active dealing with the causes

Dafür ist es besonders wichtig, eine Punishing-Mistakes-Kultur, also eine Fehler-Bestrafungs- Kultur, zu verhindern. Die Bestrafung von Fehlern fördert nur deren Vertuschung und nimmt uns die Gelegenheit, daraus zu lernen.

To really enable this we need to fight against a "punishing mistakes" culture. When mistakes are punished, they will hardly be admitted and this in turn prevents engaging with the mistake.

Goal: strengthen the safety net

For ourselves and for the system.

For everybody.

Die Devise lautet also: das Sicherheitsnetz zu stärken, die Löcher in den Käsescheiben so klein wie möglich zu machen. Für uns, unsere Organisation, für alle.

We can resemble the cheese slices again. At the top, the root, there's the organizational influences that were playing a role. This is about Regulations, culture, change management.
Then there's risk minimization. To allow for check lists, code review, pair programming or test scenarios.
Local conditions are for example fatigue, work load, lighting situation, or stress.
Individual actions now is really about the operations of an actor: Handlungen: Negligence, routine, a typo, an oversight
And eventually, and at this point we cannot prevent the accident anymore: there's the event: An outage, deleted the production database, or having a security leak.
And note the arrow on the right: the investigation path is, of course, from the event to the top where the organizational issues lie.

Organizational influences

  • Have a significant influence on the occurrence of accidents
  • Are only evident in combination with local or triggering factors.
  • Already present well before the occurrence of a recognizable accident sequence!

Wichtig ist also zu verstehen, dass die organisatorischen Einflüsse, obwohl sie tief begraben unter der Oberfläche liegen, eine wichtige Rolle darin spielen, Unfälle überhaupt zu ermöglichen. Und eben weil sie so tief begraben liegen, kommen sie erst durch einen auslösenden Faktor ans Licht. [perhaps anecdote]

Important to be aware of is that the organizational influences, even though deeply buried, take an important role in allowance of an accident. And since they're so deeply buried, they only come to light in combination with an actually triggering factor.

Anecdote

So, to make this more tangible, let me give you an example. There once was an airplane accident where the plane (one of these small ones with a single engine and propeller) ran out of fuel, the handling of the situation was poor and the plane crashed, the passengers and pilot died.

And from bottom to top, following the series of events along the investigation path, the investigators would find out that the pilot didn't bring the plane in a safe gliding attitude, hadn't have maintained a safe height where gliding to an onobstructed area was possible (because hey, a single engine can always fail), the pilot probably wasn't trained well in engine failure situation; they didn't do a proper fuel calculation, the pilot didn't check the available fuel like he supposed to, they would discover that the passengers were pushy and were distracting the pilot from his pre-check, etc etc.

Now see, many popular single-engine planes are so called high-wings. And as you might know, a plane carries its fuel in its wings.

Image by Adrian Föder, CC-BY-SA

It is also true that the fuel indicators in the cockpit are not very reliable and that's why you're supposed to literally look in the tanks, propably by using such a dip stick.

Image by Jörg Goy, https://dipstick.aero/

This job, apart from even fuelling, is not very convenient, you have to literally climb the plane. A Cessna 172 usually has small steps and a handle, but for many other high wings you would need a ladder.

And in the given case of our plane crash, there was no such ladder. The investigators conclued that the accident could have been prevented if the organisation had provided a ladder to conveniently enable the pilot to briefly sneek a peek of the fuel status.

Image by Kim Hunter from Pixabay

</ANECDOTE>

This really feels weird for how we're used to think about mistakes, and a lot of other failures have fostered the accident to happen, but eventually it boils down to the discovery of that ladder thing. Of course it's not that the gas station has been made liable: you know the invastigation is not to find somebody to blame. It's just the hidden issue in the organization, that, along with every other mistake that had a stake, eventually allowed the accident sequence to unfold.

Threat and error management"TEM"

The Professional aviation industry has visualized this with "Thread and error management".

Photo: Andy Mitchell from Glasgow, UK; CC-BY-SA

At the base, there is a Threat. A threat can occur unexpectedly, for example weather, terrain, or aircraft malfunctions.

If not dealt with properly, a threat will grow to a Crew error, caused by human action, or inaction; or bad judgement, communication problems etc. The impact of such an error can still quickly be reduced when properly managed.

However if not, it becomes an Undesired Aircraft State. Managing these kind of states are vital since if they are not they can result in serious aircraft accidents.

And now, we have an Incident or accident.

So the different stages can be mitigated. At the base of all, we can avoid already even the occurrence of a Threat by maintaining a proper health condition, training, education, check lists, and the team climate. Pilots are an a**crapful of their time in simulators and training stuff like engine failure so they can replay many of typical issues from muscle memory.

After a Threat occurs, it can be intercepted by Threat management. Recognize a threat altogether, take countermeasures and apply trained behavior.

If this leads to no effect, this means we're having a Crew error. This also must be managed of course by scrutinizing, manage workload, cross-check and the willingness to admit faults. Communication between the captain and Co is crucial.

A lot is done in crew training to also ensure that the captain doesn't maintain too much authority and allows for critic, and on the other hand that the co-pilot _dares_ to critize the captains decision.

If despite all a UAS occurs, the situation has to be stabilized, deviations must be corrected, but promptly!

TEM can be applied at any time

  • When pair programming or "pair troubleshooting"
  • "In front of the water cooler"

For yourself, keep in mind that TEM can be applied at any time! When pair programming this adresses rather the early states even before a Threat can occur; but once shit begins to hit the fan and you're in more pressure because prod is down, it's a good idea to have TEM in mind.

But even in the kitchen talking about stuff is already TEM applied.
